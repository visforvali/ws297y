# Reclaim Writing: Empty Spaces

## 10.1 Voice Chat

Welcome back, comrades!

We'll start with Perec and then move to Atwood. Some background context for both pieces before we begin: We'll be thinking about space this week, in ways designed to make it unfamiliar and strange. Space and how we interact with it lets us create our own personal sense of (in Perec's words) "the world, no longer as a journey having constantly to be remade, not as a race without an end, a challenge having constantly to be met, not as the one pretext for a despairing acquisitiveness, nor as the illusion of a conquest, but as the rediscovery of a meaning, the perceiving that the earth is a form of writing, a *geography* of which we had forgotten that we ourselves are the authors."

First, a question: What is a space? How can and do we observe spaces? How can and do we remember them? Whatever we consider a space, what determines it? Our memories, identity, and experiences? Or external definitions that have been imposed on us?

The best summary of Perec's *Species of Spaces* from which this excerpt is taken is, as Perec puts it: "The subject of this book is not the void exactly but rather what there is round about or inside it." 

Perec takes us from one space to another; in the excerpt we read, we start with the authorial space, the blank page, then the filled page; then we move to the bed, and as the rest of the book progresses, to the bedroom, apartment, building, streets, town, and so on and so forth.

The pieces reflect on space &mdash; realizing the infra-ordinary as a lens, what we notice or don't about the spaces we use or inhabit &mdash; and also reflects on spatial concepts using plain description; experimental writing; marginalia; arrangements of works on the page designed to defamiliarize it; and lots of ultra-personal and mundane aspects of spaces and their micro-histories that ultimately try to reveal that they aren't so mundane after all. 

What do you make of the transition between the blank page, filled page, and the bed? What connections might be made here between the activities we do in these spaces: i.e., think, write, sleep, be intimate, rest and and heal?

There are lots of ways to create a taxonomy, so it's maybe notable that Perec's taxonomy of space is not a straightforward set of definitions. From what he offers, what would you say has changed about how you define the page? Your bed?

The textual wandering Perec does here is like an intentional enactment of thinking about space, itself made spatial through writing about space. These are not isolated but relational definitions constructed through experience, provenance, memories. Thus, these are Perec's particular spaces based on the particulars of his life. Spaces and how we inhabit and move through them, and what we move into and around them, is what it is to live.

What does it mean, to inhabit it the space of the page? To lie in a bed?

When you write on the page, can you say you're changing space? Or what?

When you change the position of the bed, can you say the bed or room has changed?

When you write in bed, can you say two spaces have converged?

Is to mark or inhabit a a space to take possession of it? What does taking possession of a space mean? What does being displaced from a space &mdash; the page, the bed &mdash; mean?

From an imperialist/colonialist perspective, space is often something to conquer; Perec's endotic lens emphasizes curiosity, not conquest. 

When spaces are made relational like this in strange, unexpected ways, where do you locate the thresholds between them? What happens on those thresholds &mdash; pen leaving page, body leaving bed?

How do you think differently now about orienting yourself in space?

Moving on to Atwood: Theoretical fiction is one name, the most common name, for the blending of fiction and theory, which loosely describes a variety of forms and themes. They overlap in their use of the intellectual to design a fictional story and the use of the fictional to lend poetry to the intellectual. By this I don't mean to suggest that stories &mdash; our typical shorthand for fiction &mdash; aren't intellectual, but that all stories are inherently intellectual, all theory potentially poetic. Creative writing especially for social justice so often is the art of translating the theoretical, intellectual, educational, institutional, political, and discipline-specific into the poetic, imaginative, speculative, affective, and evocative.

Theoretical fiction as a genre is usually traced to French Canadian writers, and a school of Australian scholar-writers, who called it *fictocriticism*.  The name fictocriticism might make additionally apparent that the fiction genre itself becomes critical &mdash; at least for those who presume it wasn't already critical &mdash; and its critical elements become more emphasized or brought to the surface. Atwood's work uses fiction and poetics to stage theoretical questions about gender, class, race, poverty, famine, and war.

Theoretical fiction as a genre blends theoretical and fictional elements, often preserving an investment in poetics and evocation alongside intellectualizing and a frequently activist bent. All good stories are theoretical, and we tell stories and write theories to make sense of our lives.

What is gained by blending theory and fiction? Or is this a false dichotomy?

What's gained by working through any frustration or confusion that arises when reading something genre-fluid whose philosophical denseness and poetic intricacies might one or both be difficult to understand?

Theoretical fiction is women's writing, a specifically feminist project of moving away from rational masculine writing toward something more embodied, more willing to recognize the text and use it as artifice &mdash; not a one to one representation of reality. The theoretical fiction movement was essentially a theoretical project &mdash; dismantling language as one of patriarchy's institutions as used up to this moment in the late 1970s and early 1980s. The explosion of the exact verisimilitude demanded by patriarchal language; the construction of a "feminine" language linked to the body; and an increased emphasis on the participation of the reader in the co-creation of the text through the very act of reading &mdash; of interacting with the text however it invites or enables them to.

In this sense, the craft of theoretical fiction like this piece serves to deconstruct the text by deconstructing the body in order to construct new feminine/nonconforming/non-patriarchal bodies, languages, and realities via new textual forms and fictions.

Margaret Atwood's fiction is often located at the interstices of genres, especially in her early theoretical fiction work like *Murder in the Dark*. The theoretical work of "The Page" and other pieces in this collection reflect on the text's emphasis on sense receptivity and the ways the senses contribute to knowledge making in relation to the text's language, reality, and interpretive framework.

"The Page" is a game: involving artifice, a changing reality, an "eye" that isn't the organ with which we see the phenomenal world but the mind's eye, a reality made with language &mdash; whose changing nature makes clear that neither vision nor language are dependable. Other senses must be used to get at any sort of truth, and that truth is not a narrative resolution but a shift in the reader's own relationship to reality, to many realities, most of which may lie beyond their experience.

How did you find yourself interacting with and responding to this text?

What did you immediately notice about the form of this piece? How would you describe the effect of its craft? What is the relationship between its craft and its content?

What's being implied about what happens in the space of the page when one attempts to write &mdash; staring and awaiting inspiration or wounding it with the pen?

What makes the blank page so dangerous and full of darkness for writers? Is it the page that's dangerous, the writing, or the writer?

You need a certain amount of nerve to approach the page authentically as a writer &mdash; to be willing to enter dark psychological territory, which means truth more than trauma &mdash; to be able to excavate truth, ugliness, and beauty, deeper than superficial analysis or description &mdash; the blank page marks a boundary between sanity/the world we know and possession/the world that has yet to be profoundly revealed and articulated.

## 10.2 Sync Text Chat

Welcome back, comrades!

Today's class is a sync text chat, in which we'll share lines and passages during our scheduled class time that resonated with you, confused you, or that you'd otherwise like to engage with more deeply. 

Let's try close-reading the following lines! From close-reading the words being used in the lines below, what do you think each excerpt means?

**From Perec**:

- "We live in space, in these spaces, these towns, this countryside, these corridors, these parks. That seems obvious to us. Perhaps indeed it should be obvious. But it isn't obvious, not just a matter of course."
- "In short, spaces have multiplied, been broken up and have diversified. There are spaces today of every kind and every size, for every use and every function. To live is to pass from one space to another, while doing your very best not to bump yourself."
- "Before, there was nothing, or almost nothing; afterwards, there isn't much, a few signs, but which are enough for there to be a top and a bottom, a beginning and an end, a right and a left, a recto and a verso."
- "I write; I inhabit m y sheet o f paper, I invest it, I travel across it. I incite blanks, spaces ( jumps in the meaning; discontinuities, transitions, changes of key)."
- "This is how space begins, with words only, signs traced on the blank page."
- "Space as inventory, space as invention."
- "Virtual space, a simple pretext for a nomenclature. But you don't even need to close your eyes for the space evoked by these words, a dictionary space only, a paper space, to become alive, to be populated, to be filled"
- "Space as reassurance."
- "We generally utilize the page in the larger of its two dimensions. The same goes for the bed. The bed (or, if you prefer, the page) is a rectangular space, longer than it is wide, in which, or on which, we normally lie longways."
- "it's almost always a sign of a catastrophe if several people have to sleep in it. The bed is an instrument conceived for the nocturnal repose of one or two persons, but no more. The bed is thus the individual space par excellence"
- "Bed: where unformulated dangers threatened, the place of con traries, the space of the solitary body encumbered by its ephemeral h arems, the foreclosed space of desire, the improbable place where I had my roots, the space of dreams and of an Oedipal nostalgia"
- "But people no longer pay any attention to ceilings"
- "We spend more than a third of our lives in a bed. The bed is one of the rare places where we adopt roughly speaking a horizontal posture."
- "And what about hammocks? And paliasses? And bedsteads? And box-beds? And divans deep as the grave? And straw pallets? And railway couchettes? And camp beds? And sleeping-bags resting on air-beds themselves resting on a carpet of earth?"

**From Atwood**:

- "The page waits, pretending to be blank. Is that its appeal, its blankness?"
- "The page itself has no dimensions and no directions. There's no up or down except what you yourself mark, there's no thickness and weight but those you put there"
- "The page is without vistas and without sounds, without centres or edges. Because of this you can become lost in it forever."
- "If you decide to enter the page, take a knife and some matches, and something that will float. Take something you can hold onto, and a prism to split the light and a talisman that works, which should be hung on a chain around your neck: that's for getting back. It doesn't matter what kind of shoes, but your hands should be bare. You should never go into the page with gloves on. Such decisions, needless to say, should not be made lightly."
- "The question about the page is: what is beneath it?"
- "The page is not a pool but a skin, a skin is there to hold in and it can feel you touching it. Did you really think it would just lie there and do nothing?"
- "Touch the page at your peril: it is you who are blank and innocent, not the page."
