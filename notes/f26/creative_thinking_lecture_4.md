# Reclaim Learning: The Art of Curation 

Welcome back, comrades!

**Housekeeping**: Today, we'll have an async text chat to maximize your ability to post your thoughts and engage in a sort of makerspace activity as well.  

**Cvetkovich**: Today, we're continuing to reclaim how we think and reclaiming part of the research process as well! 

To pull out some important points from her talk, Cvetkovich likens the bibliographic altar to "ritual and sacred practices that render material objects full of feeling and meaning… also practices for making intangible thoughts and feelings material." At first, this altar was for her a snapshot of a particular moment in time: "A way of representing the work that went into the publication of *Public Feelings*." 

The assemblage in the first photo was put together for a book event so she could have something tangible that represented different aspects of the book, where different piles of books represent the research and personal process that went into each chapter. She wanted it to be tactile and material as well, so she added objects that have particular meaning for her like photographs, a soundtrack that plays a role in the book, a stress cat from a conference that led into the book, Virgin Guadalupe which is where the idea of the relationships between materiality and attention and the concept of the altar comes from for her.

Take a moment to answer this general question in #🙋classroom to get us started:

- As students, student researchers, and student writers, what resonated most with you about the idea of a bibliographic altar?

You can think of the process of constructing bibliographic altars as an *archival* process because it entails collecting and compiling remnants and representations of experiences. In a way, the bibliographic altar is itself "an archive of feelings." For Cvetkovich, an important part of the bibliographic altar process is finding ways to make thoughts turn into realities or theories or writing &mdash; ways of bringing disparate bibliographies together. 

There's no one way to make an altar, either: they can be 2D or 3D, assembled on a wall or a table, can involve pulling a tarot card each writing session can also be part of an altar and can help you through "stuck places" when writing.

Consider the following questions: 

- What does "archive of feelings" mean for you?
- Where is the "archive of feelings" associated with, say, the last writing project you did for a class, physically stored? Or where might you locate it *outside* of you?
- What would go into your bibliographic altar for this semester so far?

**Dr. Mani's Example**

As an example, I assembled a quick bibliographic altar for a manifesto-style position statement on inequitable labor conditions at Pace that I'm currently working on:

<img src="http://visforvali.github.io/ws297y/assets/images/bibliographic_altar_example.jpg" alt="Example of a bibliographic altar">

I included books &mdash; Mimi Khuc's *dear elia*, Lorca Garcia Pena's *Community as Rebellion*, Haytham El Wardany's *Book of Sleep*, and Sara Ahmed's *No* &mdash; along with other, non-bibliographic artifacts: my Traveler's Company planner notebook and my Roterfaden "Everything Work-Related" notebook, which basically contain and organize my personal and work life; my yellow pain journal; a paperweight made by a dear late friend, featuring a linocut of a hand holding a molotov cocktail reading "Make it accessible or burn it down - Gabrielle Peters"; a green spiky massage ball; a leather cervical spine collar; 2 photographs &mdash; one of my cat Athena, a red-and-white Siberian tabby, and one from the 2016 *Sacred Spaces* exhibit at the Rubin Museum; and 3 tarot cards &mdash; the Ten of Swords featuring a woman impaled on 10 swords and text reading "Everything Is Fine," The Chariot featuring Ares from *Hades*, and The Abyss card from Khuc's [Asian American Tarot](https://www.mimikhuc.com/projects/asian-american-tarot){:target="_blank"}.

As a 2D artifact, I've also included the following screenshot of my initial Zotero project folder, containing PDFs of articles and books:

**Makerspace Activity!**

Now, build your own bibliographic altar by selecting and meaningfully assembling objects from around your home that have informed and inspired your studies, creative directions, and lives. If it helps to narrow the variables, you can build a bibliographic altar focused on a project you're working on right now or that you recently completed. Include books, tactile objects, 2D artifacts, anything that feels like it belongs. Arrange it spatially and visually in a way that makes sense to you.

Then, answer the following questions:

- What was the process of assembling this bibliographic altar like?
- How did you decide what books to include? What tangible non-book objects would go into your bibliographic altar? What does the most central (not necessarily significant or spectacular) object in it represent?
- What changes when we make our intangible practices &mdash; like thinking or research writing &mdash; tangible and material? When we assemble these intangible practices in a sacred form, like an "altar"?
