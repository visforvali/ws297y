Today, using Ann Cvetkovich and her recorded talk on bibliographic altars as a departure point, we continue to reclaim how we think &mdash; and begin to reclaim other parts of the research and writing process as well! 

To pull out some important points from her talk, Cvetkovich likens the bibliographic altar to "ritual and sacred practices that render material objects full of feeling and meaning... also practices for making intangible thoughts and feelings material." At first, the bibliographic altar she shows us was for her a snapshot of a particular moment in time: "A way of representing the work that went into the publication of her book [*Depression: A Public Feeling*](https://www.dukeupress.edu/depression){:target="_blank"}." 

The assemblage in the first photo was put together for a book event, so she could have something tangible that represented different aspects of the book, where different piles of books represent the research and personal process that went into each chapter. She wanted it to be tactile and material as well, so she added objects that have particular meaning for her: photographs; a soundtrack that plays a role in the book; a stress cat from a conference that led into the book; the Virgin Guadalupe, which is where the concept of the relationships between materiality, attention, and the concept of the altar first emerged for her.

With that context in mind, take a moment to answer this general question in #🙋classroom to get us started:

- As students, student researchers, and student writers, what resonated most with you about the idea of a bibliographic altar?
- As students at Pace, how do you think the idea of a bibliographic altar &mdash; let alone actually constructing one &mdash; might disrupt the dogmatic image of thought that constructs how we're "supposed" to do research and writing in the university classroom?

As Cvetkovich suggests, the process of constructing bibliographic altars is an *archival* process, because it entails collecting and compiling remnants and representations of experiences. In a way, the bibliographic altar is itself "an archive of feelings." For Cvetkovich, an important part of the bibliographic altar process is finding ways to transform thoughts and theories into material, tangible realities in addition to the act of writing (which also makes the intangible tangible) &mdash; which then naturally lends itself to uniting bibliographies that aren't "supposed" to go together, that may not make sense to an outsider but make sense to you because they consist of the disparate materials that informed your thinking, writing, researching, and revising &mdash; whether they're habitual practices or idiosyncratic to a specific project. 

There's no one way to make an altar, either: they can be 2D or 3D; they can be assembled on a wall or a table; they can involve doing a one-card tarot reading every time you sit down to write to try to get through "stuck places," and then making that card part of your altar.  

Take some time to reflect on the following questions in #🙋classroom: 

- What does "archive of feelings" mean for you?
- Where is the "archive of feelings" associated with the last writing project you did for a class physically, tangibly stored? In other words, where might you locate it *outside* of you, and *outside* of the text you produced and submitted for the class?

## An Example of a Bibliographic Altar

As an example, I assembled a quick bibliographic altar for an open-access manifesto-style position statement on inequitable labor conditions at Pace that I was working on a couple of weeks ago:

<img src="http://visforvali.github.io/ws297y/assets/images/bibliographic_altar_example.jpg" alt="Example of a bibliographic altar">

I included books &mdash; Mimi Khuc's *dear elia*, Lorca Garcia Pena's *Community as Rebellion*, Haytham El Wardany's *Book of Sleep*, and Sara Ahmed's *No* &mdash; along with other, non-textual artifacts: my Traveler's Company planner notebook and my Roterfaden "Everything Work-Related" notebook, which basically contain and organize my personal and work life; my yellow pain journal; a paperweight made by a dear late friend, featuring a linocut of a hand holding a molotov cocktail reading "Make it accessible or burn it down - Gabrielle Peters"; a green spiky massage ball; a leather cervical spine collar (because I sincerely believe disability hacks/assistive items can and should be punk and fashion-forward); a photograph of my cat Athena, a red-and-white Siberian tabby, and a photograph of the ceiling from the (unfortunately problematic) Rubin Museum's 2016 *Sacred Spaces* exhibit; and 3 tarot cards &mdash; the Ten of Swords, featuring a woman impaled on 10 swords and text reading "Everything Is Fine"; The Chariot from a *Hades*-themed deck, featuring Ares; and The Abyss card from Mimi Khuc's [Asian American Tarot](https://www.mimikhuc.com/projects/asian-american-tarot){:target="_blank"} (which we'll be working with in a few weeks!).

I'm also including the following 2D artifact: a screenshot of my initial Zotero project folder, containing PDFs of articles and books:

<img src="http://visforvali.github.io/ws297y/assets/images/bibliographic_altar_example.jpg" alt="Screenshot of a Zotero folder">

And finally, as a separate image because I couldn't manage to capture it in the single photograph above, I'm including my actual cat and an orange Squishmallow that is the perfect size for supporting my head and neck when I'm resting or working in a horizontal position:

<img src="http://visforvali.github.io/ws297y/assets/images/athena_orange.jpg" alt="Orange Squishmallow resting against a red-and-white Siberian tabby">

I included the books and Zotero screenshot to identify the textual inspirations and resources for my project; the notebooks to highlight the analog components of my writing process; the therapeutic artifacts to illustrate my disabled embodiment, which inform how I write and how I feel &mdash; physically, cognitively, and emotionally &mdash; about myself while I write and about my writing itself; the paperweight, photographs, and my cat Athena to gesture at the sensory, relational, interpersonal, and interspecies experiences that also underpin my writing process; and the tarot cards to showcase some of the cards from my favorite tarot decks I pulled while working on this project. I arranged it in a way that would be easy to photograph all at once, but also intentionally placed my leather cervical collar on top of the books to emphasize how I need to support my neck even to read sometimes, put my tarot cards on top of my pain journal because they feel relevant to its contents, and left my paperweight roughly where it already exists, in a spot where I see it every time I'm working or teaching at my primary desk setup.

The process of making bibliographic altars feels physically grounding and spiritual to me, and stimulates my curiosity in ways that feel more like a "browsing" style of research &mdash; the kind I learned to do, by wandering through objects in my home or physical books in a library instead of honing in on a specific item through a "focused search" and ignoring everything else around it. I like sifting through what I own, thinking about why I own it, why I placed it where I did, where I would place it in an altar. This specific altar reminds me that I experienced a lot of pain and fatigue while writing this position statement, but also that I rediscovered wonderful texts for the project and found ways through a writing process further complicated by worsened symptoms that involved real self-care: using the spiritual and sensory (tarot, bedlife with my Squishmallows and Athena), the therapeutic (cervical collar, massage ball), and the analog and organizational (my notebook systems and Zotero workflow).

## Instructions for Making Your Own Bibliographic Altar

Now, you'll spend the rest of the day building your own bibliographic altar!

Select and meaningfully assemble objects from around your home that have informed and inspired your studies, creative directions, and lives so far this semester. If it helps to narrow the variables, you can build a bibliographic altar focused on a project you're working on right now or one that you recently completed. Include books, tactile objects, 2D artifacts, anything that feels like it belongs. Arrange it spatially and visually in a way that makes sense to you.

Then, answer the following questions with as much detail as possible (you can write your answers in your journal or type them):

- What was the process of assembling this bibliographic altar like?
- How did you decide what books to include? What tangible non-book objects would go into your bibliographic altar? What does the most central (not necessarily significant or spectacular) object in it represent?
- What changes when we make our intangible practices &mdash; like thinking or research writing &mdash; tangible and material? When we assemble these intangible practices in a sacred form, like an "altar"?
