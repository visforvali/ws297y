**"Image of Thought"**: These next two weeks, we begin to reclaim how we think!

To keep building on what we've begun to reclaim through Perec's work, what we need to question as well is how we *think*. Thinking is habitual and taken for granted despite being a fundamental activity of existence and of research and writing. When Deleuze talks about a "dogmatic image of thought," he's saying we're born into a conformist mode of thinking where thought is restricted by a normative conception of "common sense": i.e., "everybody knows what thinking means." In addition, he suggests that there's a normative conception of the thinker as being inherently moral and possessed of good will, a person dedicated to seeking the truth and working against error, when this is not necessarily the case.

So when Deleuze describes thought as a "representation," he's levying a criticism, saying that the idea of thought becomes reduced to a representation of what thought is traditionally "supposed" to be. Ultimately, Deleuze feels this normative thinker actually has "ill will" because this is a thinker who can't think in a creative, conceptual, actually natural way.

*Rhizome* gets a mention here and might help you think through these big ideas. The rhizome refers to plant roots and is defined by Deleuze and Guattari in *A Thousand Plateaus* as a horizontal, nonlinear network that "connects any point to any other point." This is in contrast to trees, which sprout from the rhizome but are hierarchical in nature. The tree, i.e. the hierarchical representation, has become the paradigmatic representation of knowledge and thought in Western epistemology (the study of knowledge).

There are intellectual and philosophical traditions and habits that establish in advance what thinking is supposed to be &mdash; but in reality thinking should be an activity that's truly creative.

As we'll further unpack later this and next week, deviating from the dogmatic image of thought opens up new possibilities for what thinking can be.
