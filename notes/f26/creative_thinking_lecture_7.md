Today, we reclaim the right to be unwell. The excerpt for this week is from Mimi Khuc's book *dear elia*, which follows a creative-critical project called [*Open in Emergency*](https://www.mimikhuc.com/projects/open-in-emergency){:target="_blank"} about Asian American unwellness, including artifacts like a poster, a tarot deck designed for Asian American and student expenses, a hacked DSM, etc. As a side note, if you're ever on the Pleasantville campus, you can swing by Choate House and ask our administrative assistant Maureen on the 2nd floor to look at a reserve copy I left with her some time ago (you can't take it out of the office, but you can flip through it in our waiting area for as long as you like!).

*dear elia* is aimed at students and professors, particularly Asian Americans and disabled folks, but also other nonwhite, non-Western, multiply marginalized members of the academic community. 

"Cura Personalis" is a speech Khuc was invited to give after being nominated to be an honorary member of Alpha Sigma Nu at Georgetown University during the third year of the pandemic. It's a significant phrase in that it's part of Georgetown's mission statement.

The central message of this speech is this: *We are not reducible to our achievements*. We are told constantly as students and faculty, especially along certain identity lines such as model minority status, that excellence is the measure of our personhood &mdash; and it isn't.

Khuc presents us with the important concept of *differential care*, or care that accounts for *differential unwellness*, meaning the ways that we experience unwellness differently and very specifically in relation to the structures we encounter &mdash; but we aren't supposed to think we need or deserve care unless you're excellent. 

"Cura personalis means you are allowed to fail" &mdash; or, rather, should be allowed to fail &mdash; as in not meet ableist, racist, classist standards of normative perfection designed to kill us. 

Reflect on the following questions in #🙋classroom:

- Do universities truly authentically commit to care in the spirit of cura personalis, i.e. care of the whole person?
- How does Pace make you feel &mdash; specifically what have they done or what structures exist here &mdash; that make you feel you don't deserve care unless you're excellent?
- What does care look like at Pace? Is it happening? Where/how or not?
- As Khuc observes, "Ableism tells us there is an ideal person, body, mind that we have to aspire towards, and any deviation from that renders us less-than, maybe even worthless." What structures of ableism, generally or specific to Pace, make you feel that being weak is to be a failure, and that to fail is to be undeserving of care?
- In what ways do the impositions of ableism, generally and at Pace cause you to deny yourself care in the sense of cura personalis? What specific forms of care do you notice you deny yourself most? If there's a hierarchy of such denials, why do you think you prioritize it that way?

And finally, I'd like to end with a larger, praxis-oriented question that Khuc asks: 

- "How might you ask the university to be accountable to the kind of mental health you want? What structures would foster this kind of mental health, and what would it take to build them?" (46)
